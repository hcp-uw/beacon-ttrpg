# 1: HOW TO PLAY

Participants in BEACON take on one of two roles: either that of a **player** or that of the **game master** (**GM**).

The goal of all participants is to tell a shared story, and to have fun doing so. Each player creates a single **player character** (**PC**) – a heroic figure called a "**beacon**" – and explores the game through that character's story. The GM, on the other hand, controls all of the **non-player characters** (**NPCs**), sets up combat scenes, and helps shape the narrative.

## CORE PRINCIPLES

BEACON has six core principles:

- 1 The well-being and fun of the group, including both the players and the GM, is the top priority.
- 2 Specific text takes precedent over general text.
- 3 BEACON does not model reality or real-life physics, and is not meant to.
- 4 Narrative play and combat play are distinct modes of play. The ability to do something in one does not automatically grant the ability to do it in the other.
- 5 In combat play, players can do anything their abilities allow, even if it doesn't make narrative sense.
- 6 Always round up to the nearest whole number.

## GENERAL TEXT VS. SPECIFIC TEXT

General text includes basic rules of the game, such as "The [[actions/fight:5 Fight]] action is used to make a melee or improvised weapon attack."

Specific text is more granular or detailed than general text. For example, the Slow tag specifies that, "Abilities with this tag can **only** be activated as part of [[actions/full attack:7 Full Attack]]." This means that a Slow weapon can't be used as part of the [[actions/fight:5 Fight]] action, because the text of the Slow tag is more specific than the text of the [[actions/fight:5 Fight]] action.

## COMBAT AND NARRATIVE SCENES

BEACON's gameplay is split into **scenes**. A scene is a series of events that focus on a person, place, or challenge. Each scene is either a **narrative scene** or a **combat scene**, falling under the rules of either **combat play** or **narrative play**.

The GM determines when one scene ends and another begins, and whether the scenes are narrative or combat. But anyone can suggest or request a scene, and you are encouraged to do so.

Combat scenes use the rules for [[combat_play: combat play]]. These scenes feature important fights and battles. Not every fight is worthy of a combat scene; only those that are important or tactically challenging move into combat play. A simple street fight or barroom brawl, for example, wouldn't start a combat scene. A fight on a crumbling bridge against a horde of demons, or a climactic showdown with an archnemesis would be worthy of a combat scene.

*Example:* An angry necromancer has performed a ritual in a nearby cemetery, creating a continuous stream of undead soldiers that attack nearby towns. The beacons are asked to stop the necromancer and break the ritual. The GM sets up a combat scene with the beacons fighting in a misty cemetery against undead hordes and a necromancer riding an animated mausoleum!

Narrative scenes, on the other hand, use the rules for [[narrative_play: narrative play]]. Any scene that isn't a combat scene is considered a narrative scene. These scenes involve challenges that are resolved by rolling dice (called "skill checks") or roleplaying. Small-scale fights can even happen during narrative play, but these fights are resolved just like any other challenge that happens during narrative play, using skill checks and roleplaying instead of the combat play rules.

*Example:* The beacons need to get to a floating island and the only way to get there is with an airship. They don't have one, so they ask the GM for a scene with the Artificer's Guild. The GM sets up a scene in which the beacons meet the guildmaster directly. The beacons attempt to convince the guildmaster to let them borrow an airship, using a mix of roleplaying and skill checks to determine how successful their attempts are.

## STRUCTURE OF PLAY

The gameplay of BEACON revolves around [[quests: quests]]. Quests are missions, tasks, or jobs provided to the beacons by the GM. Quests contain both combat and narrative scenes, connecting them to tell stories. The quests that your characters participate in form the body of your **campaign**, which is the overarching story of your characters.

For example, your group's primary motivation might be to defeat a great evil. In order to gain the strength, allies, and resources to do so, they might need to undertake a number of quests, preparing themselves for the final showdown with their foe.

The time between quests uses the rules for narrative play. During this time, the players collaboratively tell their beacons' stories and explore the world.

When the players are ready to begin a new quest, they equip their beacons with abilities they've unlocked that they think will best suit the upcoming quest and the combat scenes within it.

When the beacons return from a quest, they level up and become more powerful. This allows them to combine abilities and equipment from multiple [[classes_and_jobs: classes]] to create unique playstyles and character configurations.

## DICE

During both narrative play and combat, players roll dice to determine success, failure, and other interesting outcomes.

BEACON uses two types of dice: **20-sided dice** (:adx[d20]) and **6-sided dice** (:adx[d6]). The rules sometimes call for a **d3** (:adx[d3]), which can be represented with a :adx[d6]. A result of 1–2 equals 1, 3–4 equals 2, and 5–6 equals 3.

A number in front of the type of die indicates how many dice of that type to roll. :adx[3d6] means you roll 3 six-sided dice.

### ATTACKS, CHECKS, AND SAVES

There are three main types of dice rolls in BEACON: **attack rolls**, **checks**, and **saves**. They each use a single :adx[d20]. Any time you're making one of these rolls, roll a :adx[d20] and add the appropriate **modifiers** to the result – bonuses or penalties to the roll based on beneficial or disadvantageous situations. The final result is your **total** for the roll.

#### ATTACKS

**Attacks** are hostile actions taken against other characters in combat play. They are usually made with weapons, spells, or skills. When attacking, you add your beacon's Grit score to the roll. See [[combat_play: Combat Play]] for more information.

#### CHECKS

**Checks** are rolls to determine how successful challenging actions are. There are two types of checks that you might make: **skill checks** and **ability checks**. To make a check of either type, you roll a d20 and add any relevant modifiers. To succeed, you need a total of **10 or more** (after modifiers).

**Skill checks** are used in narrative play. If one of a character's **background skills** would be helpful for the roll they're making, or they can invoke their **title**, they can get a bonus on to the roll. See [[narrative_play: Narrative Play]] for more information.

**Ability checks**, by contrast, are used in combat play. They use the character's relevant **ability score** – Bulk, Agility, Mind, or Magic – as a modifier.

During combat play, characters sometimes need to make **contested checks**. In a contested check, the two relevant parties each roll an ability check. Whoever gets the highest total succeeds, with ties going to the one who attacked or initiated the action.

#### SAVES

**Saves** are rolls made to resist effects during combat play. Effects that prompt a save indicate which of the target's ability scores to use as a modifier. Each character has a Save Target, which is the total their opponents must meet or exceed when rolling saves. When there is no Save Target indicated, it defaults to **10**.

#### MODIFIERS

There are three types of modifiers that can affect rolls, increasing or decreasing their results:

- Ø Accuracy ( todoA )
- Ø Difficulty ( todoD )
- Ø Static modifiers (+1, –4, etc.).

Bonuses from your beacon's ability scores and Grit count as static modifiers.

### ACCURACY AND DIFFICULTY

Various effects can apply Accuracy or Difficulty to a :adx[d20] roll.

For each todoA or todoD, roll :adx[1d6] and take the **single highest result** out of the dice rolled. If rolling with , this number is added to the total as a **positive modifier**. If rolling with todoA, it is subtracted from the total as a **negative modifier**. For example, if you are rolling with 2 todoA, you would roll :adx[2d6] and keep the single highest result out of all the dice. If you rolled a 2 and a 4, you would keep the 4.

Rolls can be affected by multiple instances of todoA or todoB, although they cancel each other out on a one-to-one basis. For example, a roll could receive 4 todoA and 2 todoB; however, the 2 todoB would cancel out 2 todoA, leaving the roll with 2 todoA. Always determine the final amount of todoA or todoB before rolling any dice.

An individual roll can't benefit from more than 6 todoA or todoB.
